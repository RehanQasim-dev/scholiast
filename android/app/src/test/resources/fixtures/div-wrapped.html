Field Notes on Migratory Birds

Every September the same argument returns to the harbour town: whether the swifts leave on a date or on a whim. The bird ringers keep records; the publican keeps opinions; and both agree only that one morning the sky over the church is simply empty.

Ringing returns have mapped the routes for a century. A bird caught here in July turns up in Ghana in November, having crossed the Sahara on stored fat no thicker than the last joint of your thumb.

Weather decides the departure more than the calendar does. A tailwind on a clear night can empty the reed beds in hours; a cold front parks the birds on the estuary until the barometer turns.

What the ringers learned, and the town forgot, is that migration is a relay of stopovers. Lose one marsh and the whole route frays, because a thousand kilometres of open sea begins exactly where the reeds end.
