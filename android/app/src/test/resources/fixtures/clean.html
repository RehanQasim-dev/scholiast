The Quiet Architecture of Coral Reefs

By Marina Okafor

Coral reefs are built by animals so small that a handful of them would weigh less than a coin, yet together they raise limestone cities visible from orbit. Each polyp secretes a cup of calcium carbonate, and over centuries those cups stack into ramparts that blunt entire ocean's force before it reaches land.

The reef is an economy before it is a structure. Algae living inside the coral tissue pay rent in sugar made from sunlight; parrotfish graze the algae back so it cannot smother its landlord; and the sand on the beach below is, quite literally, ground-up reef passed through fish. Remove one trade and the others wobble.

Warming water breaks the first bargain. Stressed polyps expel their algae, and the city turns white not because it is dead but because it is starving. A bleached reef can recover if the heat eases within weeks; a second summer rarely leaves anything to recover.

What survives heat is not the biggest reef but the most connected one. Larvae drift between islands, and a damaged stretch can be reseeded by neighbours if currents and corridors remain open. Conservation, at bottom, is the practice of keeping doors open in the ocean.
